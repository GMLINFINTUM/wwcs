ℹ️ About Us

Washington Weather and Climate Services (WWCS) is a free community weather service for Washington State — built and run by volunteers who believe everyone deserves clear, local, trustworthy weather information.

WWCS brings together official National Weather Service alerts, ZIP-code forecasts, radar, volunteer spotter observations, and plain-language weather education in one accessible place. The project is pursuing a Washington nonprofit structure so it can grow responsibly and unlock grant funding for weather stations and community outreach.

The organization

Proposed legal form: Washington nonprofit corporation seeking federal 501(c)(3) public-charity recognition. Until formation and IRS approval are complete, WWCS must not represent itself as a tax-exempt charity.

Mission: WWCS improves public access to understandable Washington weather and climate information, supports volunteer weather observation, and provides practical education that helps communities prepare for changing conditions.

Vision: Washington residents can quickly find trusted official alerts, local context, and community observations in one accessible place.

What WWCS does

Weather access

Statewide headlines, National Weather Service alerts, ZIP-code forecasts, and radar.

Spotter network

Public education

Plain-language weather stories, preparedness content, and community discussion.

Why this model can work

  • The service is already tangible: the public site is live, installable as a progressive web app, and has a working Spotter Center.
  • The initial cost is low: the project can remain remote-first, volunteer-led, and equipment-light while demand is tested.
  • Microgrants fit the stage: small grants can fund formation, one station, a refurbished laptop, outreach, and basic risk controls.
  • Scale depends on evidence: volunteer activity, observations, repeat visitors, partnerships, and user feedback will drive the next funding case.
Current footing: WWCS is live at wwcs.onrender.com. The founder reported an initial traffic count of 34 on September 25, 2026. A $500 Pollination Project application was submitted on September 24, 2026; no award is assumed in this plan.

Local observations add context to official forecasts

Washington's terrain and microclimates can produce sharply different conditions over short distances. WWCS can make official information easier to use while giving residents a responsible channel for local, time-stamped observations.

The problem WWCS addresses

  • Information is fragmented: residents move between alert pages, radar, forecasts, neighborhood groups, and social posts.
  • Neighborhood detail is uneven: official stations cannot describe every hill, valley, shoreline, road, or community at the same moment.
  • Community reports lack structure: useful observations are often missing the time, place, and condition details needed for interpretation.
  • Preparedness language can be technical: plain explanations help residents understand an alert and find authoritative instructions.

WWCS should act as an access, education, and participation layer. It should link people to authoritative sources, show clearly labeled community reports, and explain that observations are not official warnings.

Primary beneficiaries

  • Washington residents: people checking weather, road-adjacent conditions, smoke, heat, wind, snow, rain, or storm impacts.
  • Volunteer observers: residents who want a simple, constructive way to contribute local weather information.
  • Community groups: neighborhood associations, libraries, schools, and preparedness groups that can use or share plain-language resources.
  • Underserved locations: communities with fewer nearby observations or less access to local weather reporting.

Public-benefit commitments

Free access

Core forecasts, alerts, observations, and educational content remain available without membership fees.

Source clarity

Official agency information and community observations are visibly distinguished.

Privacy

Collect only data needed to operate the spotter program; avoid publishing precise home addresses.

Accessibility

Use mobile-friendly pages, plain language, strong contrast, and low-bandwidth design.

Safety boundary: WWCS supplements - and never replaces - National Weather Service warnings, local emergency management, 911, or instructions from public authorities.

Three programs keep the mission focused

1. Free Washington Weather Desk

Purpose: make key weather resources easier for the public to find and understand.

Activities: display Washington weather headlines, active official alerts, ZIP-code forecasts, radar, and explanatory articles; maintain mobile and installable access.

Year-one outputs: reliable public pages; source labels; monthly content review; uptime and usage tracking.

2. Community Weather Spotter Network

Purpose: gather useful local observations while building community participation in weather awareness.

Activities: recruit volunteers, provide a reporting guide, collect time-and-place-based observations, moderate submissions, and display accepted reports.

Year-one outputs: 30 registered volunteers, 20 active spotters, 300 accepted observations, and representation from at least five Washington counties.

3. Weather Education and Preparedness

Purpose: help residents understand changing conditions and take appropriate next steps.

Activities: publish plain-language explainers, share links to official training and preparedness resources, and conduct small online or community outreach sessions.

Year-one outputs: 12 educational posts, two outreach events or partner presentations, and a reusable spotter orientation.

Quality controls

  • Label the source: show whether information came from an official feed, WWCS editorial content, or a community observer.
  • Moderate before amplification: remove unsafe, abusive, promotional, or personally identifying submissions.
  • Correct visibly: maintain a simple correction process and update inaccurate content quickly.
  • Train for consistency: use a standard field-report format covering location area, time, condition, units, and optional photo evidence.

Low-cost operating model

  • Remote-first: no office lease during year one; use online meetings and free public meeting space when appropriate.
  • Volunteer-first: no salary is budgeted until stable funding and board approval exist.
  • Separate records: nonprofit money, accounts, equipment, and contracts stay separate from personal activity.
  • Free public service: sponsorships and grants must not control forecasts, alerts, observation acceptance, or safety messaging.

Quarter 1 - Form and protect

Quarter 2 - Equip and standardize

Install the first station if funded; establish a documented observation format; publish spotter orientation; add privacy and correction procedures; recruit through community groups, libraries, schools, and weather networks.

Targets: 10 active spotters, 75 accepted observations, three counties represented.

Quarter 3 - Partner and teach

Hold or join two outreach sessions; pursue one school, library, emergency-preparedness, or community-science partnership; publish monthly weather education; test a short user-feedback survey.

Targets: 20 active spotters, 200 cumulative observations, one working partnership.

Quarter 4 - Evaluate and renew

Publish a one-page impact report; review participation, quality, and reach; file required state and federal reports; set the second-year budget; apply for larger local and regional grants using documented results.

Targets: 30 registered volunteers, 20 active spotters, 300 accepted observations, five counties, 500 people reached.

Washington residents often rely on scattered sources for forecasts, alerts, radar, and neighborhood reports. Incorporation is the first filing, not the finish line

Before filing

  • Check that the WWCS legal name is available.
  • Recruit and confirm the initial directors.
  • Choose a Washington registered agent and physical address; understand the public-record impact.
  • Choose a December 31 fiscal year-end unless the board has a reason to do otherwise.
  • Have the purpose and dissolution clauses reviewed for 501(c)(3) compatibility.

Proposed purpose clause

This corporation is organized exclusively for charitable, educational, and scientific purposes within the meaning of section 501(c)(3) of the Internal Revenue Code, including improving public access to weather and climate information, supporting community weather observation, and providing public education and preparedness resources.

Proposed dissolution direction

After payment of liabilities, remaining assets will be distributed for one or more exempt purposes within section 501(c)(3), or to a federal, state, or local government for a public purpose. Final wording should be checked against current IRS and Washington requirements.

After state approval

  1. Obtain an EIN directly from the IRS; it is required even with no employees.
  2. Hold the organizational board meeting; adopt bylaws, policies, officers, budget, and banking resolution.
  3. Open an account in the nonprofit's exact legal name.
  4. Complete the Form 1023-EZ eligibility worksheet. If eligible, file electronically; current published user fee is $275. Otherwise use Form 1023; current fee is $600.
  5. Determine whether Washington charity registration is required before soliciting broadly.
  6. Review Department of Revenue registration based on activities and revenue.
  7. Calendar the Washington annual report and federal Form 990-series filing.
Important: Washington incorporation does not automatically create federal tax-exempt status or a blanket Washington tax exemption. Do not promise donors a tax deduction until the organization has a valid basis to do so.
